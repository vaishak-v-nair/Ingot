The exam, the study pile, and the leak

An AI model is graded like a student. It sits standard exams — called benchmarks — and the scores are published and compared. But a model learns by reading a mountain of text: its training data. The mountain is too big for anyone to read, so nobody is sure whether the exam questions are already lying in it. When they are, the model has effectively seen the test in advance, and its score measures memory rather than ability. That is contamination — and a score alone cannot reveal it.

Ingot reads the mountain for you. It takes one exam and one pile of text, finds every place the exam's exact words appear in the pile, and shows you each one with the words around it — so the verdict is a judgement you make with your own eyes, not a percentage you have to trust.

  • Pick the exam. The menu above holds three public benchmarks: GSM8K is maths word problems, HumanEval is programming tasks, MMLU is general-knowledge questions.
  • Drop the study material. Any training-data file on your machine. It is read here, in this tab — nothing is uploaded anywhere, and you can watch the network panel to prove it.
  • Read the overlaps. Every match is shown in its surrounding text. Some overlap is innocent — famous quotes appear everywhere — which is exactly why you need to see the words rather than a score.

No file to hand? Watch it work walks you through a real scan step by step, and Try a sample corpus runs it directly: five ordinary documents that still produce two real matches. Reading those two teaches the most important habit this tool asks of you: seen words are evidence, a percentage is only a claim.

Why the answer is trustworthy

Your corpus never leaves the machine

Not a policy. An architecture. The page has no upload path — the file is read through the browser's own file API, hashed and scanned on your CPU, and the report is built in the tab. Open the network panel before you scan and watch it stay empty.

0bytes of your corpus sent anywhere. There is no server to send them to.
same codeas the command line runs, so a scan here and a scan in CI give the same numbers for the same bytes. A test asserts it.

Method

Index the benchmark, stream the corpus once

The obvious way to do this — take each of 14,042 exam questions and search 20 GB for it, one by one — would take days. Ingot memorises the exam instead: it fingerprints every ten-word run the exam contains, then reads your file once, checking each ten-word run that streams past against those fingerprints. One pass, no matter how many questions.

lm-evaluation-harness does the reverse — it indexes the corpus — and reports nine days on the Pile. The benchmark is the small side and fits in memory, so Ingot inverts it and makes one pass with a rolling hash.

  7.0 MB/sec single-threaded, end to end  →  20 GB in about 48 minutes

  21.4 GB of gzipped C4 shards on disk · GSM8K at n=10 · 51 minutes wall clock
  decompression, JSON.parse, hashing and match bookkeeping all included
  reproduce with: node scripts/pretraining-scan.ts

This said 37.1 MB/sec, 20 GB in 9.0 minutes until we scanned a real corpus. That figure is the scan kernel — tokenize, roll, look up — over text already read and already parsed, and quoting it as a scanning rate dropped most of the actual work. The kernel number is still the right one for judging optimisation; it was the wrong one to put here.

The field's 13-gram default is wrong

How many identical words in a row should count as a match? Too few and ordinary language triggers it; too many and short questions can never match at all. Everyone uses 13, because GPT-3 did in 2020, and we could find no systematic re-derivation since. Running 8 through 13 over a 1,000-item benchmark with 300 items planted in 6,000 documents, verbatim and again with roughly one word in eleven dropped:

n=13 leaves 6.8% of the benchmark unscannable, because items shorter than 13 tokens produce no 13-grams and nothing can ever match them. At n=10 that is 0.3%. That one is structural rather than statistical, and it is the stronger argument. On lightly edited copies n=10 recovers 81.5% against 69.6% — a real gap at 300 planted items, where the standard error is about 2.3 points — and it costs two false positives per thousand that you can read and dismiss in a second.

An earlier version of this table claimed 100% against 3.5%. That gap was an artifact of a test fixture that deleted words on a fixed lattice, which decided in advance which values of n could survive. It is written up in docs/measurements.md, because a tool that audits other people's numbers cannot quietly restate its own.

Caveats first: one corpus pair, both 2023-era, and edited copies simulated by random word dropping rather than a model rewriting text. Real paraphrase will be harder.

"2.4% of MMLU is contaminated in C4" is wrong, and that is the finding. Of 342 flagged items we inspected 200 retained matches across 120 items and found no contamination. We found NATO Article 5, Patrick Henry, Roosevelt's Four Freedoms, the Fifth Amendment, Machiavelli. MMLU asks questions about those documents, so it quotes them. C4 is web text, so it contains them. The overlap is exact, reproducible and evidentially worthless.

The standard defence does not save you. Document-frequency filtering discarded 757 matches at this scale and 0 at 356,317 documents — same filter, same threshold, so it only engages once the corpus is enormous. Even then the survivors are canonical, because a famous passage's exact ten-gram is rare: Patrick Henry's most quoted sentence appears in one document out of nine million, as give me liberty or give me death!?- Patrick Henry.

Tested properly against a corpus of different provenance — The Pile with every web-crawl subset removed, 251,488 documents of court opinions, patents, abstracts and digitised books — 67 of the 342 are confirmed canonical, 46 of them by court opinions. The other 275 are undetermined, not leaked: a control of nine unmistakably canonical items scored 1 of 9 against that reference, so absence from it carries no information. Presence is evidence; absence is a lead.

The full write-up, including the two published numbers this project had to retract, is in three ways contamination scanning silently fails.

This is also the argument for showing evidence rather than a percentage. A false positive you can see is a judgment a reader makes in one second. A false positive expressed as a rate is one they cannot evaluate at all.

Discipline

What Ingot refuses to do

Each of these was a defect first, caught by running the experiment and reading the output instead of trusting it. All eleven are written up in docs/measurements.md.

  • No silent clean result. Every report names the benchmark items that produced no n-gram at all. Nothing can ever match those, so a clean number that stayed quiet about them would be hiding the part of the benchmark nobody looked at.
  • No verdict without the text. Matches are displayed with surrounding context. Ingot reports what overlaps; whether that is leakage is the reader's call.
  • No paraphrase in the headline. Exact matching is a fact. Weaker evidence sits in its own labelled section and is excluded from the count.
  • No unmeasured claims. The false positive rate is printed next to the detection rate, from the same run.
